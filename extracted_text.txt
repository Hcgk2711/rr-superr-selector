1. Executive Summary
This report builds a data-driven system that measures how batters and bowlers perform in
high-pressure match situations relative to their own normal performance levels, and uses it to rank
the most reliable players under pressure ahead of the Rajasthan Royals’ (RR) IPL 2027 auction. The
analysis is built on the exclusive RR data pack covering 867 matches and 200,999 ball-by-ball
deliveries from IPL and T20 International cricket between January 2022 and November 2026,
spanning five IPL seasons (2022–2026) and 140 bilateral and multi-nation T20I series.
We carried out three independent pressure models based on a set of key pressure factors —
required run rate, wickets in hand, balls remaining, phase of play, knockout stage and
match-winning causality — and triangulate them into a consensus ranking. Approach 1 is treated as
the first priority: it is the baseline-versus-pressure differential model that asks whether a player
steps up when the game tightens, and it has been audited to remove statistical anomalies
(tailenders, micro-sample outliers, retired players and associate-league skews). Approach 2 is a
continuous Composite Pressure Index (CPI) that scores every delivery on a 0–1 scale. Approach 3
decomposes pressure into concrete role scenarios — death-overs finishing, chase closing, powerplay
rebuild, death-overs bowling and defending low totals.
Because Approach 1 is the first priority and has been anomaly-cleaned, the audited Approach 1 list is
the report’s lead output. The top three batters under pressure are Shashank Singh, Najibullah
Zadran and Heinrich Klaasen; the top three bowlers are Jasprit Bumrah, Matheesha Pathirana
and Arshdeep Singh. Each pick is mapped to a specific RR squad gap and auction role, so the
recommendations are not just statistical leaders but roster-shaped additions that complement
RR’s 2026 core of Jaiswal, Samson, Parag, Hetmyer, Archer, Jadeja and Curran.

2. Problem Statement & Approach Overview
The SupeRR Selector Season 2 Round 1 challenge asks us to distinguish players who consistently rise
to the occasion from those whose performances dip under pressure, and to deliver two ordered
top-10 lists — batters and bowlers — supported by a transparent methodology. Crucially, the
problem leaves the definition of pressure open: pressure may depend on required run rate, wickets in
hand, balls remaining, innings stage, closeness of the contest, or a combination of signals.
We treat this openness as a feature rather than a gap. Pressure in T20 cricket is multi-dimensional —
a No. 7 walking in at 78/5 in the 14th over faces a different pressure to a bowler defending 8 runs in
the final over. No single threshold captures both. We therefore design three complementary models
that view pressure through different lenses, then merge them so that a player must prove reliable
across all three to top the consensus list. Approach 1 is the first priority — it is audited to remove
statistical noise and its output leads the final submission. This triangulation, with Approach 1
prioritised, is the core defence against overfitting to one arbitrary definition.

3. Data & Methodology
3.1 Dataset overview
The data pack contains five interlinked CSV files: ball-by-ball delivery records, match metadata
(stage, venue, knockout flag, result), competition-team-player mappings, playing elevens, and the
Round 1 problem statement. After joining match metadata onto the ball-by-ball table we obtain a
single enriched delivery-level dataset of 200,999 balls across 867 matches. Of these, 87,687 balls
(43.6%) come from IPL cricket across the 2022–2026 seasons, and 8,806 balls (4.4%) come from
knockout-stage matches (Finals, Qualifiers, Eliminators, Semi-Finals and Asia Cup Super 4s). The
remaining 113,000+ balls cover 140 bilateral and tri-nation T20I series, giving us a deep sample of
international cricket that often precedes an IPL auction cycle.
Every delivery carries the running innings context we need to score pressure: cumulative runs,
cumulative wickets, balls remaining in the innings, the first-innings target for chasing sides, the
required run rate (RRR) and current run rate (CRR), the phase of play (powerplay overs 1–6, middle
7–15, death 16–20), wickets in hand, and a knockout flag. These context fields are the raw material
from which every pressure signal in all three approaches is derived.
3.2 Performance metrics
For batters we measure strike rate (SR = runs ÷ balls × 100), batting average (runs ÷ dismissals,
with not-outs credited as runs), boundary frequency (% of balls hit for four or six), and the uplift ratio
— pressure SR divided by the player’s own baseline SR. An uplift above 1.0 means the batter scores
faster under pressure than they normally do; this is the cleanest single signal of a clutch profile
because it controls for each player’s natural scoring rate.
For bowlers we measure economy rate (runs conceded ÷ balls × 6), wicket-taking rate (balls per
wicket), boundary-concession frequency, and two differential signals: economy improvement
(baseline economy minus pressure economy — positive means the bowler tightens under pressure)
and wicket-rate uplift (balls-per-wicket in normal play divided by balls-per-wicket under pressure —
above 1.0 means the bowler strikes more often when it matters).
3.3 Eligibility, sample sizes & fair comparison
To avoid ranking players on tiny, lucky samples we apply hard eligibility gates. A batter must face at
least 60 pressure balls (Approach 1) or 80 high-CPI balls (Approach 2), at least 120 career balls, and
have batted in at least 8 innings. A bowler must deliver at least 60 pressure balls (or 80 high-CPI
balls), at least 120 career balls, and have bowled in at least 8 innings. These thresholds are
deliberately conservative: they cut the population of 540 pressure-exposed batters down to ~41
eligible batters in Approach 1, and 434 bowlers down to ~100 eligible bowlers. All metrics are then
converted to z-scores within the eligible set, so we compare like with like.
IPL and T20I data are pooled because (a) many auction targets are overseas players whose only
structured pressure exposure is international cricket, and (b) pressure signals are behavioural and
transferable across competitions. We do however tag every ball with an IPL flag and a knockout flag,
so the role-scenarios in Approach 3 can weight IPL death-overs and knockout deliveries more heavily

4. Approach 1 — Baseline vs Pressure Differential (Audited,
first priority)
Approach 1 operationalises the user’s stated methodology: build a baseline from each player’s
normal deliveries, isolate their pressure deliveries, and measure the gap. The model asks a simple
but powerful question — does this player raise their game when the situation demands it? A player
who scores at SR 150 normally but only SR 130 under pressure is exposed; a player who rises from SR
140 to SR 200 is a clutch asset worth bidding for. Approach 1 is the first priority of this report — it
has been anomaly-cleaned (tailenders, micro-sample outliers, retired players and associate-league
skews removed) and its output leads the consensus submission.
4.1 Pressure definition
A delivery is flagged as batter pressure if any of the following hold:
  P1 — chasing with required run rate ≥ 9 (run-rate escalation)
 P2 — death overs (16–20) of a live chase with ≤ 36 runs still needed
 P3 — wickets in hand ≤ 4 and ≤ 30 balls remaining (collapse / finish-or-die)
 P4 — death overs of a knockout match (any innings)
A delivery is flagged as bowler pressure if any of:
  Q1 — defending a chase where the opposition’s required run rate ≥ 9
 Q2 — death overs (16–20) of a defending innings
 Q3 — defending with ≤ 12 balls left and ≤ 12 runs needed (last-over/last-two-over defence)
 Q4 — death overs of a knockout match
This produced 10,761 batter-pressure balls and 19,612 bowler-pressure balls — large enough
samples for stable per-player rates once the eligibility gate is applied. The non-pressure balls from
the same player’s career form the baseline, controlling for role and skill level.
4.2 Clutch Index formula (Approach 1, audited)
The audited Clutch Index re-weights the original formula to emphasise proven absolute pressure
output rather than uplift alone, which had produced tailender anomalies (a part-time bowler with a
tiny baseline naturally shows a large uplift on a few lucky balls). The revised formula is:
Batters: Clutchbat = 0.40·z(SR_pressure) + 0.25·z(Avg_pressure) + 0.15·z(Death SR) +
0.10·z(Boundary%) + 0.10·z(Volume)
Bowlers: Clutchbowl = 0.30·z(Pressure Wkts) + 0.25·z(Death dot%) + 0.20·z(Pressure Econ) +
0.15·z(Econ improve) + 0.10·z(Volume)
Pressure strike rate and pressure average carry the heaviest batter weight because a true clutch
finisher must both score fast and stay unbeaten; death SR is added so the model explicitly rewards
the overs 16–20 execution that decides chases. For bowlers, pressure wicket volume and death
dot-ball rate lead, because containment alone (low economy) without wicket-taking does not close
out matches; economy improvement is retained to reward bowlers who tighten under pressure.
Volume remains in both formulas so that two players with similar rates are differentiated by the
reliability of their sample. This is the formula behind the audited tables below.

5. Approach 2 — Composite Pressure Index (CPI)
Approach 2 replaces the binary pressure flag with a continuous score. Every one of the 200,999
deliveries receives a Composite Pressure Index between 0 and 1, built from six normalised

Players are then ranked on their performance in the top-quartile CPI balls (CPI ≥ 0.066), weighted
against their own career baseline.

performance consistently, not just on average. This is the single most important reliability check
because it detects players who inflate their aggregate with one or two heroic innings.

8.3 Limitations
  Pressure is defined behaviourally from scorecard context, not from bio-metric or situational
narrative (crowd, venue history, personal milestone). A more granular model could add these
layers.
 T20I bilateral strength is not perfectly transferable to IPL pressure — pitches, opponent quality
and role assignments differ. We mitigate by tagging IPL balls and weighting knockout and
death-overs deliveries more heavily in Approach 3, but a venue- and opponent-adjusted model
would refine this.
